September 2026 — 29-Gallon Aquarium R&D Journal

September 2026

September ended up being another important month for the 29-gallon Balance Experiment.

By this point, the aquarium was behaving very differently from the original spring baseline.

Earlier in the experiment, nitrate commonly climbed toward approximately 40 ppm within 12–13 days after a water change.

During September, nitrate stayed remarkably low for much longer periods.

The month also included:

  • Two major water changes
  • Continued Thrive S fertilizer dosing
  • The addition of Blue Diamond shrimp from Aquashella
  • Cooler seasonal aquarium temperatures
  • Continued monitoring of black beard algae
  • Vacation preparation
  • Another opportunity to observe how the aquarium behaved under reduced feeding

As always, these observations show trends within this aquarium.

They do not prove that any single maintenance change caused the lower nitrate accumulation.

September 1 — End of the August Nitrate Cycle

Nitrate:

  • Approximately 20–40 ppm
  • Definitely around 20 ppm
  • Beginning to lean darker, but still too light to confidently call 40 ppm

Top-off:

  • 4 containers

This was Day 23 since the August 9 water change.

The important point was not whether the test was exactly 20 or somewhere between 20 and 40 ppm.

The tank had now gone more than three weeks since its previous water change without clearly reaching the approximately 40 ppm range that had appeared much faster during earlier cycles.

September 4 — Nitrate Holding

Nitrate:

  • Approximately 20 ppm
  • Still in the low end of the 20–40 ppm range

The reading appeared very similar to September 1.

Rather than interpreting small color differences as a real nitrate decrease, the most honest conclusion was that nitrate was essentially holding around approximately 20 ppm.

At this point, the aquarium was approximately 26 days beyond the August 9 water change.

September 5 — Preventive Water Change

A preventive water change was performed.

This was not triggered by dangerously high nitrate.

The goal was to refresh the system after the unusually long cycle rather than continuing to wait solely for nitrate to reach 40 ppm.

Water changed:

  • Approximately 99% of sump water
  • Approximately 50% of display water

This became the new Day 0 for the next nitrate cycle.

The completed August 9 → September 5 cycle lasted approximately 27 days.

Immediately before the reset, nitrate was still only around approximately 20 ppm.

That was dramatically slower than the original spring baseline.

September 9 — Post-Water-Change Check

Nitrate:

  • Approximately 5 ppm

This initially looked surprising because the previous readings had been around 20 ppm.

The September 5 water change had simply not been entered into the log yet.

Once that maintenance event was added, the result made sense.

Approximately 5 ppm was consistent with the aquarium's normal post-water-change range.

September 11 — Top-Off

Top-off:

  • 5 containers

The sump had dropped enough from evaporation to require a substantial top-off.

At this point, evaporation top-offs were still being performed using conditioned tap water.

The planned switch to distilled/RO top-off water had not yet begun because I was still waiting to establish the aquarium's GH, KH and TDS baseline first.

Their addition also became a new livestock variable in the ongoing nitrate experiment.

Because vacation was approaching, maintaining the safer and more familiar configuration while nobody was home made more sense than introducing another experimental variable.

September 26 — Large Pre-Vacation Water Change

Water changed:

  • Approximately 99% of sump
  • Approximately 75% of display

This was intentionally a large reset before leaving the aquarium unattended.

Pre-water-change nitrate:

  • Approximately 10 ppm

Approximately two hours after the water change:

Nitrate:

  • Approximately 0 ppm

The API test appeared strongly yellow.

Because hobby nitrate testing cannot establish a laboratory-grade absolute zero, the result is best recorded as:

Approximately 0 ppm

This became the new Day 0 baseline going into vacation.

September 26 — Pre-Vacation Fertilizer

Because such a large percentage of water had just been replaced, one normal dose of fertilizer was added before leaving.

Fertilizer:

  • NilocG Thrive S
  • 4 pumps

I did NOT attempt to add two weeks worth of fertilizer at once.

The goal was simply to provide the plants with a normal dose after the large water change.

Vacation Feeding Plan

A neighbor was responsible for feeding the aquarium during the approximately two-week vacation.

Instructions:

  • Feed only approximately once or twice per week
  • Feed very small amounts
  • Do not compensate for missed days with larger portions

The goal was to avoid one of the most common vacation problems:

Overfeeding by the person caring for the aquarium.

The aquarium contains established plants, algae, biofilm and natural grazing opportunities, so there was no reason to heavily feed the livestock while away.

Later confirmation:

When I returned on October 10:

  • Fish appeared normal
  • Nitrate was only approximately 5–10 ppm

This does not prove that reduced feeding alone produced the low nitrate level, but it does show that the conservative vacation-feeding plan did not create an obvious nitrate spike.

Black Beard Algae

BBA remained present throughout September.

The important observation was that it did not appear to be rapidly spreading.

  • Remove/treat algae on removable equipment when necessary

  • Watch the long-term trend rather than reacting to every visible patch

The Biggest September Observation

The slow nitrate accumulation observed after the August 9 maintenance did not disappear after the September 5 water change.

It continued.

September 5:

  • System reset

September 9:

  • ~5 ppm

September 15:

  • ~5–10 ppm

September 22:

  • ~5–10 ppm

September 26:

  • ~10 ppm

That means after approximately 21 days, nitrate was still only around 10 ppm.

Compare that with the original spring baseline:

April:

  • Approximately 40 ppm in roughly 12–13 days

September:

  • Approximately 10 ppm after 21 days

That is a major difference in observed nitrate accumulation.

But the cause remains unresolved.

Possible contributing variables include:

  • Reduced feeding
  • Increased plant biomass
  • Refugium development
  • Mechanical filtration changes
  • Drain plumbing cleaning performed in August
  • Fertilizer changes
  • Seasonal temperature changes
  • Changes in livestock
  • Other biological maturation within the aquarium

Because several variables have changed, it would be scientifically inappropriate to claim one of them is responsible.

The important finding is simply:

The aquarium is behaving differently than its original baseline.

And because the original baseline was documented, that difference can now be measured.

September R&D Lessons

1. A Maintenance Schedule Does Not Have to Be Controlled by Nitrate Alone

Neither September water change was forced by high nitrate.

The September 5 change was a preventive chemistry refresh.

The September 26 change was precautionary before vacation.

Water changes remain useful even when nitrate is low.

2. Repeated Cycles Are More Valuable Than One Good Reading

One low-nitrate cycle could have been random.

By September, the aquarium had produced another long, low-nitrate cycle after a completely new water change.

That makes the trend more interesting.

It still does not establish causation.

3. Vacation Feeding Can Be Conservative

The fish did not require daily heavy feeding while I was away.

The caretaker was instructed to provide tiny amounts only once or twice each week.

When I returned, the livestock appeared normal and nitrate remained low.

For an established aquarium, avoiding vacation overfeeding may be more important than trying to duplicate the normal feeding schedule perfectly.

4. Seasonal Temperature Is Another Experimental Variable

The tank moved from summer temperatures above 80°F back toward approximately 76–79°F.

Future nitrate, plant and algae behavior should be interpreted with that seasonal change in mind.

5. New Livestock Should Be Added to the Experiment Log

The Blue Diamond shrimp addition may represent a relatively small bioload change, but it is still a biological variable.

Documenting livestock additions makes future comparisons more meaningful.

Going Into October

October begins with the aquarium returning from a two-week vacation period.

Questions moving forward:

  1. How quickly does nitrate rise from the September 26 ~0 ppm reset?
  2. Does the reduced nitrate accumulation continue once normal feeding resumes?
  3. How did the Blue Diamond shrimp population handle the first several weeks?
  4. Does BBA remain stable?
  5. When should daytime air-off operation resume for fall?
  6. What are the aquarium's GH, KH and TDS baselines?
  7. When should evaporation top-offs officially switch from conditioned tap water to distilled/RO?
  8. Does the aquarium continue supporting a roughly monthly preventive water-change rhythm?

The September data strengthens one of the biggest lessons from the entire Balance Experiment:

A useful aquarium baseline is not a single number.

It is a documented pattern.

Once you know how your aquarium normally behaves, changes that would otherwise look random begin to mean something.
